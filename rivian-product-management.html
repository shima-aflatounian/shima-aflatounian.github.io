The trip that never happens.

Rivian began R2 deliveries in spring 2026, bringing the brand to a buyer who is often purchasing their first EV. They bought a vehicle built for the outdoors.

Then they plan the weekend, run the charging maths, decide it's too risky, and take the other car. Nothing is logged. Nobody finds out.

01 — The problem

A capable vehicle used at a fraction of its capability

What stops a new owner from taking the trip they bought the vehicle for?

The obvious answer is range. The evidence points somewhere else. Across EV owner communities, complaints cluster on charger reliability and availability — the broken or occupied station — rather than on insufficient vehicle range.

That distinction matters enormously, because range is a hardware problem that takes years and billions to move. Uncertainty is a software problem.

The failure is silent. A trip that was cancelled during planning produces no event, no support ticket, and no data. It is the most expensive thing Rivian cannot see.

03 — The journey

Six tools, one spreadsheet, no trip

There is no product for this, so owners assemble one: native navigation, a charger app, a forum thread to ask whether a specific station actually works, a weather app, a campsite app, and notes to hold it together. People don't build spreadsheets for problems that are solved.

Where the trip dies Framework

Confidence through the planning journey. The drop is at contingency, not routing.

Intervening earlier is premature — routing already works. Intervening later is too late — the decision has been made. Contingency is the moment.

04 — The product

Plan for the charger that isn't there

Every EV routing tool answers where. None answers what if. Trailhead plans the failure before it happens.

Contingency planning

Every charging stop carries a named alternative within reachable range, and states how much buffer remains if the primary fails. No stop is allowed to silently lack one.

Confidence in words

Not a percentage. One sentence naming the single largest risk: "one stop on this trip has no alternative within range." Specific enough to act on.

Delivery-day onboarding

The owner plans one real trip with a specialist before they leave with the vehicle. Highest-intent moment available, and currently unused.

Intent capture

Plan creation records an intended date. Departure is tracked against it. This is what makes the invisible failure measurable for the first time.

What the experience looks like

Three screens — plan, spot the risk, see the fallback Concept

Low-fidelity on purpose. The point is the information hierarchy, not the visual design: the fallback is never hidden behind a tap.

Screen 3 shows the honest case: a stop where the fallback exists but leaves no margin. The product's job is to say so before departure, not after.

What it would take to build

Trailhead is not a thin layer over existing navigation. To produce a fallback that anyone should trust, it needs vehicle state of charge and consumption history, route data with elevation and forecast temperature, charger location and connector type, and — the hard one — charger reliability and availability, either live or modelled from historical uptime. Rivian holds the first two natively through vehicle telemetry; the third comes from network partners; the fourth is the dependency that determines whether this is a one-quarter build or a one-year one.

That dependency shapes the roadmap above. It is the reason live charger status sits in Next rather than Now, and why the first release must be honest about data age rather than implying a certainty the underlying feeds cannot support.

Why Rivian should care commercially

The North Star is deliberately behavioural rather than financial. The commercial bridge, stated as hypothesis rather than measured effect:

Confidence → utilisation → satisfaction → advocacy and retained value

  • An owner who completes trips uses the vehicle the way it was sold to them, which is the condition for satisfaction rather than a synonym for it
  • Higher utilisation is the precondition for accessory attach — a rooftop tent sells to someone who camps, not to someone who intends to
  • Owners who take the trips are the ones who recommend the brand, which matters disproportionately for a company still building consideration against incumbents

Each link is a hypothesis. I have no Rivian internal data on any of them, and the first release should be instrumented to test the first link before anyone argues for the last.

Deliberately not building

A social feed, gamified trips, or a separate app. The first two are adjacent and move nothing. The third puts a download in front of a product whose entire purpose is removing barriers.

05 — Measurement

One number that cannot be gamed

North Star

Multi-day trips completed per owner, first 12 months

Not app sessions. Not planner opens. Not time in app. Those measure whether a product is interesting. This measures whether it worked — and it cannot be moved by making the product stickier.

07 — Experimentation

The decision rule, written before launch

Primary experiment

Randomised at owner level, at delivery. Control gets routing and charging stops. Variant gets the same plus named fallbacks and a confidence rating. Primary metric is plan-to-departure rate within the stated date window. Minimum 90 days — trip planning is seasonal, and a two-week read would be noise.

Decision rule Agreed in advance
ResultAction
Plan-to-departure +10pts, no guardrail breachShip to all deliveries
Positive but under 10ptsIterate on the design, re-run
No differenceStop building. Re-open discovery.
Written down in advance so it cannot be renegotiated later by whoever is most attached to the feature.

The 10-point threshold is a starting position, not a statistical claim. The final minimum detectable effect and required sample size would be set once baseline plan-to-departure behaviour is observed — which is itself one of the reasons the first release functions as a measurement instrument before it functions as a feature.

A qualitative round runs first — eight to ten moderated sessions testing whether a trip rated "one stop has no alternative" prompts re-planning or abandonment. It is the highest-risk unknown in the design and the cheapest thing to check.

08 — Go to market

Nobody has to buy it. Someone has to open it.

Trailhead ships with a vehicle the customer already bought. That removes acquisition entirely and replaces it with adoption — which moves the budget from media to the delivery experience.

The narrative

Not "new trip planning features" — that's a changelog, and nobody's behaviour changes because of a changelog. The story is that the vehicle was always capable of the trip; now the plan is too. Completing a promise rather than adding a function.

The 90-day question

Did plan-to-departure move? If yes, expand. If no, stop and re-open discovery.

10 — What I learned

Measuring something that doesn't happen

My first instinct was that the R2's problem was price — the $45,000 promise arriving three and a half years late. That is real, but it is a marketing problem, and I had been asked for a product one. The distinction took me longer to see than it should have.

The hardest part was measurement. A cancelled trip generates no event. The whole product exists to prevent a non-occurrence, which is close to unmeasurable. Capturing intent at plan creation — asking when someone is thinking of going, then checking whether they went — was the only mechanism I could find that makes the failure visible. It is imperfect, and I would rather have an imperfect number than an honest blank.

What I would do differently: talk to owners earlier. Everything here is built from public sources, which is honest but thin. Ten conversations would have strengthened the central hypothesis or killed it in a week — which is exactly why the qualitative round runs first in the experimentation plan.
