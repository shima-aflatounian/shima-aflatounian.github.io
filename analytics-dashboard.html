Performance Monitoring & Early-Warning Signals

This dashboard was designed to help teams monitor operational performance over time and quickly identify where attention or intervention may be required.

  • Tracks backlog and processing-volume trends across the last 12 months
  • Highlights increases and decreases using clear visual trend indicators
  • Compares performance across 1-day, 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year timeframes
  • Surfaces unfavorable movements so teams can prioritize the areas requiring management attention
  • Helps managers focus recurring meetings on the most important issues instead of manually reviewing every metric

The capacity section compares remaining versus used capacity to help identify operational pressure early. Forecast views extend the analysis by estimating when facilities or processes may reach capacity constraints, allowing teams to plan ahead rather than react after a threshold is reached.

Backlog Burn-Down & Capacity Planning Model

This is a hypothetical dashboard created with dummy data to prepare for a client project before kickoff.

The business question was: given a large backlog and a fixed completion deadline, is the current processing rate sufficient to reach the target — and if not, how much additional throughput would be required?

  • Shows current progress against a fixed business deadline
  • Compares actual performance with an ideal completion trajectory
  • Estimates whether the current processing rate is sufficient to reduce the backlog to the target level
  • Quantifies how much additional capacity or throughput may be required when current performance is insufficient
  • Supports management conversations around planning, execution, resourcing, and delivery risk

The goal is not simply to report current performance. The dashboard is designed to support a management decision: whether the team is on track, off track, or needs to adjust capacity and execution to meet the target.

Scanner Fleet Capacity & Investment Analysis

Operations wanted to buy additional production scanners. The stated reason was capacity: volumes felt heavy, the floor felt busy, and the newest units were visibly faster than the oldest ones.

Before approving spend, the question worth answering was narrower than the one being asked. Not "how many scanners should we buy?" but "is throughput actually the binding constraint?" I built a production dashboard across the ten-scanner fleet to find out.

  • Tracks model, effective pages-per-minute, active days and page volume for every scanner in the fleet, split by document type
  • Normalises a mixed fleet onto a single throughput unit so ten different machines can be compared on one scale
  • Trends rolling twelve-month average volume at fleet and scanner level
  • Forecasts the scanner-equivalents required under a range of growth assumptions, and reports the shortfall against the current fleet

The forecast is parameterised rather than fixed: growth is a slider, so a reviewer can stress the assumption themselves. The shortfall stays at zero across every plausible growth rate, which is a far more convincing answer than a single number produced by an analyst.

A finding that removed metrics rather than adding them. The timestamp underpinning every hourly throughput figure turned out to be a bulk system commit time, not the moment a page was actually scanned. Pages-per-hour and utilisation were therefore not measurable from this source, and I retired them from the executive view rather than let a plausible-looking number carry a decision it could not support.

What the analysis concluded

The fleet was not capacity-constrained. Current demand consumes roughly four units of ten, twelve-month volume is declining rather than growing, and the forecast shows no shortfall under any reasonable growth assumption. Buying additional units would have added throughput the business had no work for.

The real case was a fleet refresh and consolidation: retire the two oldest, slowest units, concentrate volume on the current-generation machines, and reduce the number of assets under maintenance. That is a smaller cheque and a different business case than the one originally proposed — and it came from reframing the question rather than answering the one that was asked.
