American Express · Digital workplace products

Digital product adoption & self-service analytics

American Express ran two conversational assistants for internal technical support: one embedded in the chat tool colleagues already used all day, and one inside the dedicated support portal. Both were positioned as self-service products. Neither was behaving like one.

I analysed several hundred thousand assistant conversations to answer a product question rather than a reporting question: where in the journey do users stop trying to solve their own problem, and which of those drop-off points can the product actually fix?

01 · The product question

Adoption was fine. Completion was not.

Volume was high in both channels and had been for months, so the programme was reported as healthy. But the product almost never finished the job it existed to do — and nothing in the existing reporting made that the headline.

4.5%of support-portal assistant conversations resolved without a human
11.1%of chat-embedded assistant conversations resolved without a human
~2 in 3portal conversations handed over to a live agent
~1 in 3chat conversations abandoned before any resolution

A self-service product that resolves under 12% of contacts is not a self-service product — it is a queue with extra steps in front of it. And the lighter-weight channel resolved roughly 2.5× better than the purpose-built one, which pointed the investigation at friction and context rather than at content coverage.

02 · The journey

Two failures that look the same in a report

Before segmenting anything, I mapped the support journey end to end so every metric had a place to sit. That made something obvious: existing reporting measured entry and volume well, and measured the middle of the journey — the part where the product either works or doesn't — barely at all.

Escalation — a cost problem

The user gets an answer, just not from the product. Expensive, but the need is served and the outcome is recorded.

Abandonment — a product problem

The need walks away unmet and untracked. This was the larger risk and the one nobody was reporting on.

03 · Segmentation

One product, two entirely different audiences

Both assistants were built as one experience for one audience. The usage data disagreed. I derived a tenure band for every conversation, and channel preference turned out to track strongly with it: newer colleagues concentrated in the chat-embedded assistant, while the portal drew a materially more experienced population.

Newer colleagues don't know the support system, so they ask wherever they already are. Experienced colleagues go to the portal because they already know the assistant probably won't help and they want the ticket. Same product, two different jobs being hired for — and the portal's much lower resolution rate is exactly what you'd expect if users arrive already intending to escalate.

04 · Intent

Separating a failed product from an unwilling user

Outcome alone couldn't distinguish "the assistant failed the user" from "the user never intended to use the assistant." So I built a classification that reads the opening turns of each conversation — whether the user described a problem or went straight to asking for an agent or a ticket — and crosses that with how the conversation actually ended.

Roughly three quarters of conversations in both channels contained a genuine, valid issue. The self-service product was failing real needs, not being worked around.

The portal inverts the chat channel: nearly half of its conversations disengage before the assistant gets a chance, against 31% in chat. Combined with its older-tenure user base, that is the signature of a channel users have already learned not to trust.

05 · Recurring needs

What users were actually asking for

To understand the content side, I clustered conversation text to group recurring requests instead of reading them one at a time. The method matters less than the result: demand was heavily concentrated, and it was concentrated in exactly the categories a well-built assistant should own.

This separates the backlog cleanly. The first two clusters are knowledge and flow problems the assistant can be built to solve. The third is a capability gap: no amount of better content resolves a request that needs a system action the product cannot take.

06 · Product decisions

What I recommended

Change what the assistant is for, by channel

Stop shipping one experience to two audiences. The chat-embedded assistant should be optimised for newer colleagues and first-time problems: guided resolution, plain-language prompts, obvious next steps. The portal should stop pretending an escalation-intent user wants a conversation, and instead route them faster while capturing structured context on the way through.

Build depth in the top two clusters before adding breadth

Access and connectivity concentrate enough volume that a genuine end-to-end resolution flow for those two categories moves the resolution rate more than broad content expansion across every topic.

Treat abandonment as the priority metric, not escalation

Escalation is expensive but the user is served. Abandonment means the need walked away. Roughly a third of chat conversations ended that way, and nothing in the existing reporting made it visible.

Instrument the middle of the journey

The stages between engagement and outcome were effectively unmeasured, so it was impossible to say whether users abandoned because the answer was wrong, slow, or never offered. Without those events, any improvement can be shipped but not evaluated.

Close the loop where the product cannot act

For provisioning-type requests, the honest product decision is a fast, well-labelled handoff rather than a failed self-service attempt that costs the user time before escalating anyway.
