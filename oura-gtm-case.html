Every rival ring is now free. So what is the membership?

Samsung, RingConn, Ultrahuman and Amazfit all ship with no ongoing fee. Oura charges $5.99 a month on top of a $349 ring.

The obvious read is that Oura is the last company charging rent. Its owners say something different — and the difference is the strategy.

The hardest competitor is the person who reads every comparison, decides none of them justify the price, and keeps wearing the watch they already own.

The strategic bet

We are betting that the membership is bought as a service relationship rather than as access to data, because the sensors have converged and what is left to differentiate is interpretation and what happens when the hardware fails.

  • Why now: every credible rival went subscription-free, so the fee can no longer be defended as the category norm.
  • What we are deliberately not doing: competing on sensor counts, chasing elite athletes, making medical claims, or dropping the fee to match.
  • What proves it right in 90 days: members who receive a replacement or an anomaly alert renew at a materially higher rate.

Screen-free guidance, not another tracker

Fitness tracker. Competes on sensors, metrics and accuracy claims — a fight where everyone now looks the same.

An ongoing sleep and recovery guidance service, worn without a screen, that turns overnight signals into one clear action each morning.

For people who want better sleep and recovery without wearing a screen, Oura is a passive guidance service that turns overnight signals into one clear action each morning. What we deliberately do not say

  • "More accurate." Owners say sensors are comparable. This invites a fight we'd lose and don't need.
  • "Only $5.99 a month." Minimising the fee concedes it's a cost rather than a service.
  • "Unlock your full data." Exactly the paywall framing that makes the fee feel like a hostage situation.
  • Anything medical. Wellness guidance, not diagnosis. This is a hard line, not a preference.

Module 5 — Pricing & Packaging

Stop selling access. Start selling the service.

Pricing goal: defend a position, not maximise revenue. The fee is not too high — $160 over three years is a small gap. The problem is that nobody can tell what it buys.

Three-year cost of ownershipPublished prices

The gap is smaller than the objection suggests.

Recommended packaging

Hardware purchase includes twelve months of membership. Basic tracking stays permanently. Guidance, trends and support renew annually. This removes the feeling that an expensive ring turns into jewellery when you stop paying — the objection that kills the sale at the shelf.

Research I would commission

Conjoint analysis, because the real question isn't "what price" — it's which combination of hardware price, included months and feature split people actually choose. Van Westendorp would only tell us what feels expensive; MaxDiff would tell us which features matter. Neither answers the trade-off.

The assumption being tested: that including twelve months raises year-two renewal rather than simply delaying the same churn.
